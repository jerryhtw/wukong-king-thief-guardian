In short

What it is. A short action game in three chapters. In each one you walk Sun Wukong through a painted town seen from above, talk to two residents, then step into a gold ring and the camera dives through mist to his shoulder for a 3D boss fight. It ships as an Android APK.

How it was made. Almost all of it happened inside one Claude Code session. I made the decisions, generated the images, models, music and video in other tools, and played every build. Claude models (Fable 5.2, Opus 5.5, Sonnet 5.5) worked as a crew of agents that planned, designed, wrote all the C++, built the arenas, ran the tests and packaged the game.

What this page is. The full record, in order, including the plan I threw away, the ideas I rejected, an engine install five agents broke between them, and a tutorial that sat stuck on my phone for 204 seconds. Times are Korea Standard Time and come from the commit history.

35 hfrom the first session to the final build
97 minfrom greenlight to a playable first chapter
266commits across the game and docs repositories
39 → 60frames per second on the test phone

I confirmed the final build at 02:57 on Monday 16 November, about eight hours before the official deadline. It ships as an Android APK, with a Windows build made from the same commit. QA and packaging lists what was checked on it and the two issues I know it still has.

The three bosses

Almost every boss attack draws its shape on the ground before it lands, and each boss draws in his own colour and manner. Learning a boss mostly means learning to read his marks.

One chapter, start to finish

The Morning Drill. The game opens on the waterfall ledge with a wooden training dummy. The drill teaches one thing per step, in the order the first boss will ask for them: move and look, the three-hit combo, dodging off a red mark, the perfect dodge and its counter, the big attack, clones, and finally a dodge through the water curtain, with gold arrows on the ground pointing the way. On a phone the big attack is the ultimate itself, played in full on the dummy; on PC it is the Focus Smash, and the PC drill adds a heavy-attack step. The drill can be skipped.

The town. Each chapter then starts in a painted town seen from above, live from its first frame. You walk Wukong among residents who are busy with their own day, with the stick or by tapping where you want to go: the tap ripples and a gold ring marks the destination. A large gold exclamation mark shows who to talk to next. There are two conversations per town. Each gives a line of story, a hint about the boss or a boon for the fight (clone charges, longer warnings, more health, lanterns), and the task list ticks as you go. When both are done, a gold ring opens at the edge of town, under a beam of light and a pointer that are hard to miss.

The crossing. Step into the ring and cross. The camera dives through mist to Wukong's shoulder, the boss gets his reveal and name card, and the fight begins in 3D.

01

The bet

Saturday 14 November · 15:38

I am a solo developer, not a student, and I started late. The jam opened on Thursday afternoon and this log begins on Saturday at 15:38, which left under two days.

Why Wukong? Black Myth: Wukong inspired me deeply, and it was a big part of the motivation behind this plan. Inspiration is where it stopped. The novel is in the public domain and that game is not, so from the first hour the rule was to learn from its genre and never borrow from it (see section 03).

The bet was that I could spend those days directing instead of typing. I would hold the decisions that need taste, money, an account or a submit button. Everything else would go to Claude models running as agents inside a single Claude Code session, each used where it is strongest. Fable 5.2 took concepts, story and art direction. Opus 5.5 took planning, the core game code, debugging and the job of orchestrating the others. Sonnet 5.5 took high-volume building: arenas, effects wiring, document revisions.

One rule from my brief shaped the whole project: do not run every stage on the same model at the same effort. It wastes time and tokens. A one-off plan synthesis got Opus at maximum effort. A master-plan revision got Sonnet at default.

02

The first plan, and why I threw it away

Saturday 23:00 → Sunday 00:12

My first brief asked for too much at once: a high score on all seven judging criteria, clever use of sponsor products, an Android client talking to a dedicated server on AWS, a 2D part mixed with a 3D part, an East-Asian myth, and a mechanic nobody had seen.

Before any design, Sonnet research agents checked each assumption against primary sources: the 19-page official rules, the jam page, engine and plugin release notes. They wrote one shared fact file so later agents would not research the same things twice. The most useful part was a table titled "your assumption vs reality". AWS is not a judging sponsor. Several NVIDIA and AWS plugins I had counted on have no UE 5.9 build. All text and voice must be English. Sponsor usage does not change the score, and ties are broken by re-scoring Fun at double weight.

That last line mattered more than anything else in the file.

At 00:06 Opus wrote a master plan with a model and effort matrix for each stage and seven checkpoints for me, named after film production, from G0 Greenlight to G6 Final Cut. Six minutes later Fable delivered three concepts built around the jam theme.

The three pitches were well argued and they left me cold, so I greenlit none of them, and the eight-hour night shift planned for the agents never ran. The reason was that they were written to fit a theme. At 01:05 I wrote new directions into the brief and handed them over at 09:57 on Sunday morning.

03

The pivot: make it fun, make it Wukong

Sunday 09:57 → 12:03

The new brief was shorter and blunter. Set the theme aside and make the most fun game possible, since Fun is the tie-breaker and a forced theme was hurting the ideas. The subject is Sun Wukong, a myth everyone knows, so a five-to-ten-minute game can start fast. AWS is out. The deadline is 22:00 tonight. And before any concept art is paid for, show me ten cheap concepts.

I also asked for a AAA-style third-person action feel for the 3D half. The crew drew a line before doing anything else: study the grammar of the genre (lighting, camera, dodge-first combat, boss pacing) but never the characters, bosses, levels or UI of Black Myth: Wukong or any other game, and never feed anyone's media to a generator. The jam forbids third-party IP, and copying would have put the entry at risk. One of the ten concepts was later cut because its setting sat too close to an existing title.

While Fable wrote concepts, Opus checked my keys with free calls and found a money problem. The 3,200 Tripo credits from the jam perk live in Tripo Studio, the web app. The API wallet read zero, and the two are separate purchases. Opus estimated $10 to $20 to top up. I declined and decided to do the generation by hand in Studio from prompts the crew would write. That made me part of the critical path, which I accepted.

Two misreads in twenty-five minutes

I told the crew the 2D part should be a top-down town that lives on its own, in the spirit of the simulated town from the Generative Agents research paper (Park et al., 2023): residents with routines, talking to each other in passing. That made the five side-view art styles Fable had prepared irrelevant.

Fable re-ranked the concepts and chose number four, a village where a demon hides among the residents. I stopped it at once. I did not want a deduction puzzle set in a town. I wanted the simulation itself.

No. Different from #4. It must move like that town.My note at 11:05

Instead of guessing a third time, the crew asked three questions in one dialog. What does the player do there? Show me options as moving mockups. Do residents think with a live language model? No, write their routines and lines in advance. Whose town? All three: the monkey kingdom, the Heavenly Palace, and a village on the road west.

Then I test-generated the first art prompts and found two more problems. The 2D art looked like a comic next to the photoreal 3D, so a switch between them would feel like changing games. And the hero did not read as Wukong. Fable had given him granite skin and gold seams to avoid the risk of photoreal fur, which was a production convenience bought with the protagonist's identity. I asked for a furred Monkey King built from the novel's own items: the golden fillet, a vermilion vest, a tiger-skin kilt, the gold-banded staff.

04

The art pipeline

Sunday 11:45 → 13:12

The pipeline has four steps and a person in the middle of it. Fable writes prompts. I generate reference images with GPT image generation. I turn the hero, bosses and weapons into 3D models in Tripo Studio and rig them there. The crew imports them, retargets animation onto them, and puts them in the game.

Fable's prompt pack had 22 copy-and-paste prompts with settings and file names: the hero from three angles, a 3D key frame, aerial plates for each town, resident sheets, three bosses, arenas and props. I ran the whole pack in about five minutes and came back with 24 images.

Cutting 48 residents out of grey

The resident sheets came on a grey background, and a plain colour key tore holes in every grey sleeve and every patch of grey fur. Opus switched to keying by connectivity. Only grey that touches the image border counts as background, and a flatness test catches enclosed pockets such as the gap between a carrying pole and its bucket. A later pass fixed the village sheet, where speckled holes remained, by lowering the tolerance and closing small gaps.

The hero, by hand, in Tripo Studio

I built the hero myself between 12:20 and 12:35. Multiview-to-3D turned out to need a subscription, so the model came from the single front image. One pass gave the Monkey King a perfectly human face. After several rounds I switched on every quality option the free tier offers, re-topologised to 10,000 triangles, and auto-rigged with the UE5 Manny skeleton.

That last option was the most valuable click of the weekend. Opus inspected the exported file before it went near the engine: one mesh, 9,506 triangles, one PBR material, and 80 joints named exactly like Unreal's own mannequin. A skeleton with those names can use the engine's animation library through IK retargeting. It had no tail bones, so the tail follows the hips, and it was a metre tall and facing the wrong axis, both fixed at import.

Motion from Mixamo

Tripo's own animation presets cover eleven basic moves, so signature attacks had to come from somewhere else. Opus chose clips from Mixamo's library and mapped each to an attack in the fight data. Two-handed weapon sets read well as staff fighting. The final count is 54 clips, retargeted from Mixamo's skeleton to Manny to our characters by a pipeline that runs entirely from the command line: import, IK rig, retargeter, verification. They are sourced assets and are declared as such.

The first retarget bent Wukong's feet backwards. The fix was to drive the legs with plain forward kinematics from the bind pose and switch off the template's foot IK.

05

First light

Sunday 11:03 → 13:40

The Unreal project was created at 11:03, an hour before I had chosen a game. A Sonnet agent bootstrapped it from the C++ Third Person template while design was still being argued, because none of that work depended on which concept won.

Fable, meanwhile, wrote the game as data so that engineers could build systems before content was final. The design document came with six JSON tables: towns, landmarks, walkable graphs, residents with schedules, conversation lines and boss fights. Every position is a normalised coordinate read off the actual painted plate. There are 263 subtitle lines across 125 resident conversations, all written in advance.

The slowest thing all weekend was the first package. A source-built engine compiles the whole engine for the game target the first time: 1,066 compile actions took 1 hour 45 minutes, and cooking took another 36. Starting that on day one, before there was a game, is the only reason a build reached me by early afternoon. The template alone packaged to 0.68 GB, which made size a constraint from the first hour.

At 13:35 the Opus gameplay lead committed the jam game, and by 13:40 Chapter I played from title to results card. That is 97 minutes after greenlight. All of it is C++ driven by Fable's data files, with no Blueprints.

The most useful thing the lead built that hour was not a feature. One command-line switch makes the game play itself and save a screenshot at every notable moment. From then on, agents could check their own builds without a person at the controls, and I could check their claims by looking at pictures.

06

"The town isn't fun"

Sunday 13:47 → 16:05

The first packaged build with all three chapters reached me two hours ahead of the plan. I played it for ten minutes and wrote the bluntest note of the project.

The sprites don't animate, and the town gameplay is not fun at all.Dailies note, 13:47

The design read well on paper. Each town had its own verb (command the monkeys, steal from the banquet, help the villagers) and what you earned there became your resources in the fight. In the hand it was a chore between you and the boss. I asked for the town to become a short walk and a few conversations, and for the fun to live in the fight.

Fable rewrote the towns as quest hubs within the hour. Each has a few residents to talk to; each gives a story beat, a hint about the boss, or a small boon such as longer attack warnings. The residents kept their daily routines, now as ambience. The resource grind was retired.

My notes on the 3D half were just as direct: the feet bend wrongly, the staff is not really held, the hits feel rough. The lead turned its effort to the fight.

Three arenas in an afternoon

Sonnet built the arenas as separate maps streamed into the fight: a waterfall ledge, a palace terrace above the clouds, a frozen river at night. The trick that made this possible in hours was to hang my generated key frame as a matte painting behind the boss and build real 3D only in front of it. All three ran at 145 to 170 frames per second on the PC.

They also had obvious problems. Spheres, cones and box tables stood in for props. The fight camera let the hero cover the boss. The boss sometimes turned his side to you.

Packs I already owned

The launcher could not add marketplace packs to a source-built engine project, but its download cache already held packs I owned: 61 magic effects, a lightning set, a basic effects set and 50 sounds. With two CC0 sound packs from Kenney, that was enough for real sparks, slams and impacts without buying anything.

07

Three bosses who do not fight alike

Sunday 16:30 → 18:40

The release candidate at 16:29 passed a full self-played run, and I raised the bar anyway. The three bosses shared one rig, one animation set and one AI with three sets of numbers. That was the plan, and it showed. I wrote that they fight alike.

Fable redesigned them from character and weapon, and by 17:32 the lead had it in code. Each boss now reads his own section of the fight data.

The Demon King became a slow brawler and the only one who lies: he will start one attack and finish another. The Marshal never feints and keeps a strict beat, so you can learn him like a song. The River King glides without moving his legs, waits longest and strikes fastest. Their moves, and how each one is warned, are listed in What you play.

Around the bosses the crew added a reveal before each fight, a camera push-in on charged attacks, a gold glint along the staff while a perfect-dodge counter is ready, and one change to the towns that I had not asked for but liked at once. The flat hero sprite was replaced by the real 3D Wukong, rendered onto the painted plate, so the same character walks in both halves of the game.

08

Sound: Suno, Kenney, and three voices from NVIDIA

Sunday 12:45 → 17:12

I made the music in Suno from prompts Fable wrote. The pack describes twelve instrumental cues built around one pentatonic motif. The main theme carries it; the opening, the three boss themes and the ending were to be made from that theme with Suno's Cover feature so the melody follows the player through the game. No prompt names an artist or a work.

I delivered seven tracks in the first batch, in time for the 15:07 build, and the crew normalised them to a common loudness before import.

Sound effects come from two CC0 packs by Kenney, 182 sounds converted to WAV, plus the 50-sound pack from my own library. Which sound and which effect play for which event is not in code. It lives in one data file that maps an event name to an asset, a scale and a colour, with per-boss overrides, so an integrator agent could tune the feel of every hit while the gameplay lead stayed on stability.

The boss voices are synthetic and I want to be plain about that. A Sonnet agent called NVIDIA's Magpie TTS directly over gRPC to generate 43 English voice assets: seven spoken lines per boss, plus attack, hurt, roar, death and laugh sets. Each boss was then shaped differently: a cave growl for the Demon King, a voice inside a helmet for the Marshal, and an icy two-voice whisper for the River King. In the 18:33 regression run, thirteen voice lines fired per fight.

09

Running the crew, and what broke

All day, worst around 21:30

From early afternoon there were usually four or five agents working at once. An Opus orchestrator held the conversation with me, split my notes into briefs, and reviewed results. Under it sat a gameplay lead with its own sub-agents, an arena builder, an audio and effects integrator, and at times a writer or a researcher. This is how that was kept from collapsing, and where it nearly did.

What held it together

Each note of mine went to exactly one agent, and no fix counted as done until the orchestrator had looked at screenshots of it in a real fight. Shared facts lived in files, not in anyone's memory. Design reached code only as data: Fable edited JSON in the docs repository, and a sync step copied it into the game, so a designer and an engineer never edited the same file.

Commits were small and written as explanations. That paid off at around 17:00, when the gameplay lead ran out of context after four hours and dozens of features. Nothing was lost. A fresh Opus lead read the commit log and the README and carried on from the next line. Small, well-described commits turned out to be an agent's long-term memory as much as mine.

Only one Unreal editor can safely build at a time, so agents took turns with a lock. When the Android work began, that agent got its own copy of the repository so its long builds would not block everyone else.

One engine, five agents

The worst hour of the project came from an attempt to save time. Five agents shared one engine install, and the orchestrator suggested a build flag that skips the engine's check against two builds running at once. Two builds then raced on shared engine files and changed the engine's build ID. After that, every test game stopped on a version-mismatch dialog that nobody was there to click, and one agent waited 45 minutes for its turn to build. The fixes were plain: one build at a time, release the lock only after the game has exited, check the build IDs before launching anything, and a written order of turns.

A permission I had to grant

The session ran in an automatic mode in which a permission check reviews what agents try to do. When an agent went to fix a broken weapon material, the fix lay in a folder outside its brief, and the check stopped it. The orchestrator brought the question to me, I read what the agent wanted to change, and I approved it.

10

Playtest notes, before and after

Sunday 18:33 → 23:35

The build from 18:33 passed everything a machine could check. The self-play bot walked all three towns, crossed each threshold and won all three fights without dying. The logs showed no errors. Then the agents looked at their own screenshots, and the pictures disagreed with the logs.

The staff and all three boss weapons were flat grey. The Chapter I floor was covered in black torn-paper shapes. One marketplace effect filled the screen with untextured orange balls. Chapter III had dark boxes beside the boss. None of these fails a test. All of them ruin the picture.

I played that build and sent three rounds of notes over the next three hours. These are the ones with evidence on both sides.

Grey weapons

The weapon material loaded a default texture from a path that does not exist in UE 5.9. It failed to compile on the newest shader model and quietly fell back to the engine's grey default. The fix was three null defaults, committed at 19:12, and it is the fix that needed my approval in the previous section.

"The bosses hold the blade, not the handle"

The Marshal carries a great sword. The import script guessed which end of a weapon is the head by its width, took the wide guard for the head, and put his hand 54 centimetres up the blade. A per-weapon grip offset and flip in data fixed it, and the orchestrator checked more than forty close-ups across all three bosses. My next note was that the weapons were still not held straight, so the weapon axis now follows the line of the knuckles of the closed fist.

"It looks like paper stuck right behind it"

I was right, because it was. The backdrops were a ring of flat painted cards a few metres past the arena edge. Sonnet replaced them with one continuous panorama cylinder 170 metres across, with real cliffs, roofs and willows in the middle distance. You can see the difference in the first pair of images in this section.

Black rocks you could stand inside

I reported that characters could fight inside rocks. The lead added proper sweeps and push-out to character movement, which was needed, but the real culprit was stranger. The five boulders in Chapter I were inside-out meshes. They drew as black half-shells with no matching collision, so they looked like rocks you could walk into because, in every sense that mattered, they were.

The camera inside a rock

My note was not to pull the camera in when something is in the way, because the sudden zoom was worse than the obstruction. The camera arm now keeps its length and whatever comes between the lens and Wukong fades out. The first version missed one case, which the telegraph agent's own screenshots caught: if the camera started inside a boulder, the sweep reported no hit, and the screen filled with blurred rock.

"The warnings on the ground look cheap"

They were built from engine primitives: fans of flat planes, rings of 32 cubes, flattened cylinders. An Opus agent replaced the lot with one flat quad per warning and a procedural material that draws the shape mathematically, with a look per boss. A follow-up made the Marshal's gold translucent after a large warning turned the white marble into a white screen.

"Particles should make sense"

Marketplace explosions shrunk to two percent of their size rendered as balls. The staff trail was a string of 22-centimetre spheres. The integrator built a gallery map that shows every mapped effect at its real in-game scale, then went through all of them. Broken ones were removed and the rest became native-size sparks, frost and seal bursts.

"Make the tutorial fun"

The original prologue taught dodging and nothing else. Its replacement, the Morning Drill, teaches every skill on a wooden training dummy in the order the first boss demands them. Small monkeys lob peach stones at you, and two marshals heckle from behind what they insist is a very strong table. It landed at 21:43 as a nine-step drill, and it is the part of the game that changed most once it met a touchscreen.

11

21:00: the mobile pivot

Sunday 21:00 → Monday 00:36

An hour before my own deadline I changed the platform. The game would enter as an Android build, with a test on my phone by 23:00. It was the riskiest call of the weekend. The target was the jam's Pocket Powerhouse category, and the 18:33 Windows build stayed on disk as a fallback.

An Opus agent took the port in its own copy of the repository. It added a touch layout that drives the existing gamepad input, so no gameplay code had to learn about fingers. It caught one trap before I could: on-screen buttons re-send "pressed" every frame while held, which would have replayed dialogue and attacks at sixty times a second. The first engine compile for Android took 27 minutes. An APK existed at 22:06, and a 408.6 MB build with all the evening's fixes at 22:19.

I had called that one final. Then I installed it.

What the phone said

The agent drove the phone over adb and saved a screenshot at each stage of every round. There were six rounds between 22:55 and 00:06. The first one failed on the title screen.

The tutorial that stalled

This is the bug I would least like a judge to have found. On my phone the drill's heavy-attack step never completed, because the touch layout could not aim it the way the step expected. A global safety timer was meant to rescue stuck players. After 204 seconds it did, by skipping straight to the end of the tutorial. Dodge, perfect dodge, smash and clones were never shown.

The lead replaced the global timer with a per-step pass, added aim assist on the dummy, and then cut the heavy attack from the phone entirely. Phones got four actions: attack, dodge, a one-press smash and clones. The drill stopped showing keyboard key caps to a device with no keyboard, and a SKIP TRAINING button appeared for anyone who wants to go straight to the boss.

Thirty-nine frames, then sixty

The agent measured performance on the phone itself, a Galaxy Note20 Ultra, by launching self-played fights over adb. The Chapter I boss fight ran at 39.1 frames per second at native resolution. Rendering the 3D view at 80 percent brought it to 60.1 with a worst frame of 16.8 milliseconds. The foggy Chapter III was already at 59.6. With the later two-handed staff IK the fight holds 58 to 59. The setting is Android-only; the PC build is untouched.

Other phone notes from the same hour: hits lacked punch, so hit-stop got longer and the phone now buzzes on contact; Wukong slid when he ran, so his animation rate now follows his ground speed; text overflowed its boxes, so boxed text shrinks to fit. Each of these has a commit and a round of screenshots behind it.

Still not right, so back to concepts

Even at round six the touch UI felt like a PC interface that had been made to fit. My own rule is that design options arrive as pictures, not descriptions, so at 00:09 the crew delivered a page of sixteen mobile-first mockups in phone frames drawn over real device screenshots: four options each for fight controls, town, tutorial and menus. I picked one for each of the first three and rejected all four menu designs, backgrounds included. Four new ones were on the page at 00:31.

The same playtest produced gameplay cuts. The Demon King's health was halved because he was far too hard for a first boss. His shield attack looked odd and was removed. Town dialogue went from 54 lines to 18. The peach heal was replaced by a dodge with three stamina charges, where a perfect dodge refunds its charge. The SMASH button became an ultimate, and its cinematic, Pillar of Heaven, landed at 01:30.

Rounds three and four

I played each new APK as it arrived and sent notes while the next one built. A build took about three minutes by then, so the loop was short.

Round three, 01:41, on build r7. I had reached the middle of the second boss.

  • Light attacks felt slow. They are now twice as fast.
  • Mashing attack shook the camera without pause. Light hits no longer shake it.
  • The drill said to dodge through the water curtain and I could not tell where that was. It got a marker.
  • In town I wanted to see where a tap would send me, a bigger exclamation mark, and an obvious door to the boss. All three went in.
  • The Marshal held his sword at a slant and hit too hard. The grip was straightened, and he dropped to 60 percent of his health and 70 percent of his speed.

Round four, on builds r9 and r10.

  • The curtain marker had grown a glowing bar, a ring and a label. I asked for the arrows only.
  • Each town opened by darkening the screen to announce the chapter. I had it removed, so the town is playable at once.
  • In the opening, the River King's flash showed him still walking in. It now uses the close-up where he has arrived, breathing frost.
  • The ultimate's pillar left Wukong's hands on the way down and read as a throw. At 02:39 I asked that one hand stay on it. That was the last change, committed at 02:43.

Build r11 was on my phone at 02:47. I called it final at 02:57.

Cutscenes from Higgsfield

The cutscenes are mine, made with Higgsfield's image-to-video while the agents were fixing rocks. The crew's part was to make that as quick as possible for someone who had never used the tool under pressure.

QA and packaging

Final build · Monday 02:57

Testing on this project had three layers, and each caught things the others could not.

The game plays itself. A command-line switch runs any chapter unattended, with a strong setting that lets the bot win. It checks that the flow never dead-ends and that the logs stay clean of errors and asserts. It was run before every build that reached me.

Agents read their own screenshots. Each run saves a frame at every attack, warning and card. Purpose-built variants followed: weapon-grip close-ups, an effects gallery, phone-resolution layout audits on the PC, and a mode that opens each menu so text can be checked for overflow. Grey weapons, black floors and the camera inside a rock were all caught this way, with green logs.

I play it. Nothing above found that the town was dull, that three bosses fought alike, or that a phone tutorial could not be finished. Those took a person.

Packaging

The Android build is not made by hand. A cycle script checks out an exact commit in the Android copy of the repository, compiles, cooks, packages, and then inspects the APK: package name, 64-bit ARM, the data inside the file, a size between 300 MB and 1 GB, and the baked command line. Only if all of that passes does it replace the last good APK and its install notes. A three-line engine patch stops development builds opening a profiling port at start-up, which on Windows would have greeted judges with a firewall prompt.

The final build

Both submitted builds come from one commit, made at 02:43. The Android APK is the eleventh phone build, r11. It passed the script's checks, was installed on my phone at 02:47, and is the build I confirmed as final. The Windows build, play8, was packaged from the same commit and passed an unattended smoke run through the drill and into all three chapters' fights with no fatal errors, failed checks or errors in the log. Each build went into its own zip for the submission: 462 MB for Android, with a 481.6 MB APK inside, and 839 MB for Windows.

Checking the camera, not just the game

The gameplay video was captured from the final Windows build by a crew agent, and capture needed its own QA. Every take is recorded from the game window only, with the game's own audio and nothing else from the PC, and each take is scanned automatically for black frames before it can be used.

That scan exists because of a false alarm. I saw the screen blinking in a recording of the town and reported it as a bug. It was not in the game. The first screen grabber was catching empty buffers from the window, and a different capture method recorded about 1,650 frames of the same town without one black frame.

The second finding was quieter. Every game an agent launched for testing had been silent, because Unreal mutes a window that does not have focus. No agent had judged sound by ear, only by events in the log, so nothing had been missed, but the video would have had no game sound.

Known issues

The scan did find one real fault. At the cut from a boss's intro movie to the fight, a single black frame sometimes appears, more likely when the movie is skipped. It was found 25 minutes before my cut-off for the build, and the crew's orchestrator left the transition alone rather than change it that late.

Focus has no use on a phone. The gauge still fills, but the moves that spend it were cut from the touch layout and the ultimate has its own gauge. Chapters II and III also had less of my time on the phone than Chapter I.

What I would tell myself on Saturday

  1. Ask for ten cheap options before one expensive one.Three polished concepts cost me a night. Ten rough ones with mockups took 39 minutes and got me to a decision before lunch.
  2. Ten minutes of play outranks an hour of specification.The resource-town design survived every review and died the first time I held a controller.
  3. Put the two art styles side by side before committing to either.One test image settled what paragraphs of description had missed.
  4. Do not trade the protagonist's identity for production convenience.A stone monkey was easier to render. He was also not Wukong.
  5. Green logs are not a picture.A self-played run with zero errors shipped grey weapons. Someone, human or agent, has to look.
  6. A flag that skips a safety check moves the cost to someone else.Skipping the build lock saved seconds and cost five agents most of an hour.
  7. Small commits are an agent's memory.When the lead ran out of context, its successor resumed from the log without a handover.
  8. Start packaging on day one, and test on the target device as early as you can.The first package took over two hours. The first phone test took one minute to fail. I should have had a phone in my hand before 22:55.
  9. Check the camera before blaming the scene.The blinking town was a capture fault, and the silent test runs were a window without focus. Both looked like game bugs until someone checked the tool.
